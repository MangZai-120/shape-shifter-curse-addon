# Spinning the Moon

*Author: Unknown*

---

Long, long ago, there was a valley the moon loved far too much. The grass there was silver-white, and in every dewdrop hanging from the blades, a small piece of the moon had come to live. Travelers passing through called it the "Silver Thread Valley" — yet the old folk nearby spoke that name on only one occasion: the Night of the Full Gaze, the night when the moon hung roundest and brightest, in the instant the mountain wind suddenly fell still.

On the Night of the Full Gaze in one such year, a merchant caravan, racing to deliver its goods over the mountains before winter sealed the passes, decided to cross the Silver Thread Valley by night. Before departure, the old merchant leading the caravan stood before the altar of the Moonwatch Order for a long while, and in the end, still dropped a copper coin into the offering box. The old priest keeping the gate lifted his eyelids a little and said something that left everyone baffled:

"The moon only lights the road — not those who stop walking it."

They did not understand, and entered the valley all the same.

The moment they reached the valley mouth, the moon rose.

That night's moon was absurdly large. It hung low, directly above the valley mouth, like a silver eye looking down upon the small caravan. Moonlight fell and paved the whole valley into a carpet of silver, and even the moss trampled by hooves gleamed with a cold sheen. The old merchant lowered his voice and repeated his warnings over and over: "Keep your heads down, follow the lead, and don't look at the moon. On the Night of the Full Gaze, the moon eats people."

But there are always doubters in a caravan. The young hand bringing up the rear snorted, lifted his face, and had himself a good, long look at that eerily enormous moon.

He looked for a long time. So long that the mule bells ahead had begun to fade before he came back to himself.

Then he saw it — a silver thread hanging from his own shoulder. Fine and bright, like spider silk, swaying gently in the moonlight, as if someone had just drawn a strand from the moon. He reached to pick at it — the thread was icy cold, cold enough to numb his fingertips, as if the moonlight had been twisted into a solid cord.

He did not cry out. Perhaps he could not; perhaps he dared not. He simply stood there, watching himself being wrapped up bit by bit, starting from his fingertips, forming a cocoon. The cocoon soon began to come undone, until he became strand after strand of silver silk, drifting toward the moon, gently, slowly.

By the time the caravan noticed one of their own was missing and came back with torches, there was nothing left in the valley but a stretch of quiet silver grass, and a small spider web hanging from a blade of it. There was no dew on the web — only moonlight. And in its center dwelt a point of brighter light.

The second to vanish was the mule driver. He had kept his head down the whole way, counting mule hooves, and when he counted to the three hundred and twenty-seventh, the moonlight suddenly flickered.

The rein in his hand had, at some point, turned into a silver thread. Soft, and long, stretching straight into the depths of the dark. He pulled back along the thread — twice — and from the other end came a gentle answering pull, as if someone far away was reeling in the line with perfect calm, slowly, steadily, like sewing an old garment. He let go, turned, and ran. But the silver grass at his feet seemed to come alive, blade by blade coiling around his boot cuffs; and from overhead a brighter thread dropped, softly looping his wrist, the way a mother takes her child by the hand, leading him step by step back toward the deepest moonlight at the valley floor.

Those who passed the valley floor later would see, upon the stone walls, a faintly glowing gossamer thing — a web, studded with fine, glittering beads. The locals never touched it. They only said those were not dewdrops, but the stitches left behind when moonlight was woven in.

The fattest man in the caravan was the owner of this cargo. What he carried this trip was the finest snow-moth silk of the mountains; woven into snow-moth cloth, each bolt could fetch half a city tower. He did sums the whole way, counting how much this trip would earn him, his eyes gleaming brighter with every figure.

But as he walked, the crate suddenly seemed to grow lighter and lighter. He stopped, lifted the lid, and froze —

The snow-moth silk was gone. A full crate of it, all vanished, leaving behind nothing but a crate of thread, cold and weightless as moonlight. He grabbed the end of the skein and kept pulling it out, backward, without stopping.

The more he pulled, the more he panicked; the faster he pulled. The thread had no end. The silver silk he drew out piled itself upon the ground like a bolt of brocade being unraveled. He tore out the last strand in a frenzy, gasping, and looked down at the empty crate bottom —

There, across the wooden boards, in silver thread, someone had embroidered a crooked line of small characters:

"What the moon wants is more than the silk in your crate."

His head snapped up. In the valley's moonlight, the rest of the caravan stood quietly in a single, straight row, heads bowed, motionless. From every shoulder hung a silver thread, stretching away into the darkness the moonlight could not reach. At the valley mouth the mountain wind circled, like some ancient, invisible spinning wheel — creak, creak, creak.

And far away, in the deepest, brightest pool of moonlight at the valley floor, a figure older than the moon sat in perfect stillness.

It was not spinning cloth. It was spinning moonlight.

It spun the moonlight into thread, wove the thread into webs, and spread the webs across the whole valley. The webs were hung with people. They hung there quietly, like segments of moonlight, motionless. And still it wove on, stroke after stroke, patient as though tomorrow would never end.

Hugging his crate, he fled headlong out of the valley mouth, collapsed behind a great stone the moonlight could not reach, and gasped for breath. He dared not look back, and dared not look up at the sky. He could no longer remember his own name — no, he remembered; only the name kept slipping through his heart, quick as a smooth fish, impossible to catch.

There were two moons in the sky.
